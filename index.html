What does it actually take for design innovation to build lasting power?

I've spent years inside design innovation — asking big questions, running discovery, building things slowly in regulated spaces, watching good ideas stall. When my team closed, I finally had time to sit with what I'd learned. And the book that reframed almost all of it wasn't a design book. It was Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers — a strategy book. Not because design needs to become strategy, but because innovation without power is just a really good idea that goes nowhere.

These are my reflections. And I have three questions for you.

In the months since Falck closed down its Global Innovation team, I've been given the glorious gift of space and time for reflection. I loved our team, the work, and my colleagues. I loved being in-house and having the mandate to ask big questions and support teams over years as they built new things and explored new ideas. As in any innovation role, there was a lot that was uphill — thinking five, ten years ahead of teams focused on the next contract and the immediate operational needs in front of them.

One of my favourite lessons in design came from Edward Mazria — a titan in sustainable architecture and my mould-breaking boss at Architecture 2030. He taught me that an architect makes thousands of decisions at the same time, and that it's the sum of all the inputs and heuristics an architect has learned that help them make a first sketch. And that it is much easier to fine-tune a sketch that gets 80% right than to iteratively improve something that misses the ballpark.

Hamilton Helmer was talking about something similar — but for living products and businesses. Organisms that keep changing and evolving. And a few things really stuck.

Reflection One

The Art of Scouting

Great inputs for radical innovation are about studying the flex of threats and external conditions — technology, legal, competitive, and beyond. One of the values I know our Innovation Team brought was an external lens: outside-in innovation. We did it project by project, but turning this into a more ongoing craft — endlessly collecting inputs, endlessly learning — would be even more powerful.

In healthcare, obviously patients matter. But I think our team could have been even more on top of technology scouting and regulatory changes. The insight public affairs had — and learning how we could even propose changes to reimbursement regulations ourselves — really shifted my idea of what good outcomes for healthcare innovation could look like.

What does a design innovation team that is truly good at scouting look like? One that uses its collective spidey-senses to deeply understand the landscape it's operating within — shifting the very boundaries of a system?

What does a design innovation team that is good at scouting across the full landscape of change in a system look like? Have you seen it done well?

Reflection Two

The Muscle of Making

This is my favourite part of the book. Strategy isn't just innovation and then execution — it's a living process that requires you to choose the right type of innovation for the moment, then take the risk of trying and iterate. We have to make sketches, evaluate, then iterate again. It fits well with my deeply ingrained CIID mantra: Build, Test, Repeat.

And it makes a lot of sense for industries like digital products where it's easy to mock-up ideas. But this gets very, very difficult in regulated or physical spaces. It's just expensive and slow. At Falck we explored a mental health product for bystanders at the scene of emergencies. Within months we had completed our discovery research and shaped a very simple solution. A simple A/B test in a digital product takes days. For healthcare, it took a full year to get ethical approval. By the time the ten-week pilot was over, the team had been working for years on a very simple solution — without the bandwidth to take learnings and start another cycle.

If I'm being critical: the biggest failing I see in innovation is treating it as a full handover rather than an ongoing design process. The teams that do it best keep iterating. They build a dedicated muscle for making.

What can we learn from organisations that are good at taking risks, sinking resources into exploration, and iterating in slow or complex spaces?

Reflection Three

The Power Lens

I'm no stranger to business strategy, but I've never read a book that framed things through power. Lasting cash flow that makes the investments in risky innovation worth the effort. This was the most eye-opening part of the book — and I have reread it multiple times to hear the arguments from different angles.

It's how I wish I had framed some of my foresight questions before. If this innovation pays out, will we achieve lasting power? The example that stuck was Netflix. A designer could identify many opportunities to improve the UI or UX — but from a strategic point of view, that's easy to copy. What was most strategic for Netflix was to invest in their own content, because they could spread that cost across a large user base in ways emerging competitors could not.

Good inputs aren't just a deep understanding of customers — they require a highly tuned strategic understanding of where the market is, operating costs, profit margins, competitors. I would have reframed my communication to stakeholders very differently.

One thing was missing from the book that I keep coming back to. I believe we need power to make change in our world — but I was a little unnerved by the pure pursuit of cashflow. Some decisions should also be made from a moral angle. Yes, Netflix has been remarkably successful. But they are also proud of people binge-watching and getting less sleep.

It's one of the thousands of decisions a designer needs to incorporate into their choices — and again why good inputs and good evaluation are critical. I'd love to see more designers talking about why they decided not to build something, because it could have repercussions. That there are some things we need to take more slowly. Especially in this fever pitch of AI, and with the ever-looming, slow-moving catastrophe of climate impacts: moral strategy is as important as ever.

The double diamond. But the teams that are most successful are doing both sides of the triangle constantly — with a highly strategic and moral lens.
