How does design innovation build lasting power?

An interactive reflection on design innovation by Francesca Desmarais.

One of my favourite lessons in design came from Edward Mazria, a titan in sustainable architecture. He taught me that an architect makes thousands of decisions at once—that it's the sum of all they've learned and memorable heuristics that produces a sketch. And that it's far easier to fine-tune a sketch that gets 80% right than to rescue something that misses the ballpark entirely.

I've carried that lesson into design innovation. And over time I've come to believe that what separates innovation that sticks from innovation that stalls isn't the quality of the ideas—it's the infrastructure around them. Three capacities that live outside the process itself. Get these right, and your sketch has a fighting chance.

In the months since my innovation team closed, I've been given the glorious gift of time to reflect. I've wandered the forests near my house listening to books, worked through job interviews, and had conversations about what innovation looks like across different organisations.

What has sparked my biggest reflection was Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers—a strategy book, not a design book. It gave me a lens I realised I'd been missing: a rigorous framework for how companies build long-lasting power by inventing new products, processes, business models, or brands. It fundamentally changed my position on innovation.

These are the three takeaways that have stayed with me. They're still jagged, unfinished thoughts—reflections in progress. For now, each one ends with a question for you. I'd love your input.

Spark One

A Living Map

Design innovators are tasked with finding opportunities—but Helmer takes this one step further and asks practitioners to look for power openings. These are rare, highly dependent on external forces, and almost always emerge in moments of high flux: regulatory shifts, technological leaps, market disruptions.

But you can't see a power opening if you're not already watching the landscape. Long before the first sketch, design innovators need the best possible inputs. A solid understanding of what competitors are doing, how customer behaviours are shifting, where technology is heading, what external threats are forming, what capabilities are being built internally. This isn't discovery-phase work. It's an ongoing craft.

There are many tools for this—signal scanning, foresight, Wardley mapping, competitor research, system mapping, scouting start-ups, human-centred research, etc. The key insight for me is that none of these can be limited to the beginning of a project. Exploration and learning is a constant.

An ongoing living map does two things: it builds a mental library of design inspiration that can be creatively unleashed in future projects, and it keeps you alert to power openings as they begin to form.

What has worked for you? Which practitioner or organisation inspires you with their craft of ongoing exploration?

Spark Two

Muscles for Making

This was my favourite take away from the book. That strategy is a living, active process—very much like design. Helmer is a firm believer that you make strategy through action. It requires a company to take real risks, invest in uncertain pathways, and learn from how the landscape responds. Not once. Continuously.

That means the gap between "making a prototype" and "making the actual thing" is an illusion. I've seen brilliant innovation teams produce genuinely exciting ideas that went nowhere—not because the ideas were wrong, but because the organisation had no muscle for making them real. And an innovation that can't be built can't build power.

Organisations sometimes turn to a separate innovation lab, a partnership, an external consultancy as 'safe spaces' to explore and innovate—but in my experience, this might be missing the point. Maybe one of the best ways to de-risk innovation is to have it more connected to strong operational capabilities. My key takeaway was that design innovators need to be embedded in a part of the organisation that makes things. No handovers to other silos. More ongoing collaboration with makers. I've seen this work really well when design innovators lead early exploration, and then embed themselves in the teams operationalising the first working ideas—and when there is consistent leadership buy-in from the beginning.

This kind of capacity requires real investment—in budget, in organisational structure, and in the slack to take risks and learn from them. It's not a mindset shift. It's a commitment.

Do you know of teams where design innovators are working directly alongside operations—from early idea through to launch? I'd love to hear examples.

Spark Three

The Power Lens

I'm no stranger to business strategy, but I'd never encountered a framework that cuts quite this ruthlessly. Helmer outlines seven types of power—each one a combination of benefit for the holder and barrier for challengers. The argument is simple: if an innovation doesn't unlock one of these, it probably won't build lasting value.

For design innovators, this reframes the evaluation question entirely. It's not just "is there a market for this?" or "does this provide value for customers?" It's "where is this organisation in its growth cycle, and which powers could this innovation actually unlock?" That's a harder, more honest question—and one I wish I'd been asking earlier in my career.

Helmer's example that stuck with me was Netflix. In creating a fantastic experience for customers, a designer could identify many opportunities to improve the UI or UX—but many design decisions would be easy for a competitor to copy. What was most strategic for Netflix was to invest in their own content, because they could spread that cost across a large user base in ways that emerging competitors simply couldn't afford. The designer needed to frame the right UX question for strategic impact: what kind of content do a large group of our subscribers want to watch?

The power lens isn't a gate-check at the end of a project. Like the map and the making, it needs to be woven into the ongoing work—a shared language between design innovators and the leadership teams guiding strategy.

Helmer's framework changed how I think about innovation. But there was something missing for me, something that sits underneath all three of these capacities.

Power is a vehicle. The question is what's your destination.

Helmer's lens is trained on lasting cashflow, but the most powerful innovations I've seen don't just build value for one organisation. They shift something bigger. Ideas don't live in a vacuum. The organisations that move the needle on hard, systemic problems rarely do it alone. They're embedded in coalitions—public sector, private sector, communities, researchers—each contributing what no single actor could bring by themselves. Mission-driven innovation is its own discipline, and it's one I think we need more of.

So alongside the map, the making, and the power lens—I'd add a fourth capacity: a mission worth building power for. And the coalition to pursue it.

For me, the mission has always been the climate transition. My years in healthcare reinforced it: sick people and sick systems are more brittle, less able to make the beautiful, adaptive leaps that a changing world demands. The organisations I most want to see succeed are the ones building toward a future that's better for more people.
